\section{Introduction and Background}

Reinforcement Learning is an approach to artificial intelligence that works without fixed input-output ground truths. Instead, an agent can learn to make decisions by continuously interacting with an environment.
In this loop an agent observes a state from the environment, based on which it chooses its next action. This leads to the environment transitioning into a new state and responding to the agent's action
with a reward indicating its value.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      node distance=2.5cm,
      box/.style={rectangle, draw=black!80, thick, minimum width=2.8cm, minimum height=1.2cm, rounded corners},
      arrow/.style={-{Latex[length=2mm]}, thick, black!80}
    ]
    \node[box] (agent) {\textbf{Agent}};
    \node[box, right=of agent] (env) {\textbf{Environment}};
    \draw[arrow, bend left=40] (agent.north) to node[above] {action} (env.north);
    \draw[arrow, bend left=40] (env.south) to node[below] {state, reward} (agent.south);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{The reinforcement learning loop: the agent chooses an action, and the environment responds with a new state and a reward.}
\end{figure}

This reward signal can be used to optimize the agent's policy for taking actions, enabling the agent to learn.

In this project, PPO was implemented from scratch to solve the CartPole-v1 environment. Beyond training a working agent, the goal is also to study how choices of different parameters affect training stability and final performance,
such as the clipping range, the advantage estimator (Monte Carlo returns versus Generalized Advantage Estimation with varying \(\lambda\)) and the rollout length/update epochs ratio.

Each configuration is tested across multiple seeds and evaluated on a fixed, reproducible set of episodes, allowing conclusions about performance and stability to be drawn.

\subsection{Environment}

Formally, an environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). At each discrete time step \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), the environment is in a state \(S_t\), the agent selects an action \(A_t\), and (for \(t \neq 0\)) receives a reward \(R_t\). The environment then transitions into the next state, where the resulting state and reward are governed by a transition probability distribution \(p(s' \mid s, a)\) and a reward function \(r(s,a)\).

CartPole-v1\footnote{Gymnasium: \url{https://gymnasium.farama.org/environments/classic_control/cart_pole/}} models a pole attached to a cart moving along a frictionless track. The goal is to keep the pole balanced by applying a force to the cart, either to the left or to the right.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{CartPole.png}
  \caption{The CartPole-v1 environment: a pole attached to a cart moving along a frictionless track.}
\end{figure}

The environment is specified as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Action space} --- \(0\) (push left) or \(1\) (push right)
  \item \textbf{Observation space}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Cart position \(\in [-4.8, 4.8]\)
    \item Cart velocity \(\in (-\infty, \infty)\)
    \item Pole angle \(\in [-24^\circ, 24^\circ]\)
    \item Pole angular velocity \(\in (-\infty, \infty)\)
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reward} --- \(1\) per time step while the episode has not terminated
  \item \textbf{Termination}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Pole angle exceeds \(\pm 12^\circ\)
    \item Cart leaves the area \([-2.4, 2.4]\)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Proximal Policy Optimization}

PPO\footnote{Schulman et al., \emph{Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms}, arXiv:1707.06347v2 (2017).} is an on-policy actor--critic method. It alternates between \emph{sampling} with the current policy and \emph{optimizing} a surrogate objective on the collected data. The \textbf{actor} $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ chooses actions and is the policy being improved; the \textbf{critic} $V_\phi(s)$ estimates the state value and is used to compute advantages $\hat{A}_t$. Each rollout is reused for several mini-batch epochs, but the clipped objective keeps the new policy close to the one that collected the data.

PPO does not search over actions directly; it changes the network parameters so that the policy distribution assigns better probabilities. The PPO paper writes objectives to maximize; in our implementation we minimise the negative objective with Adam:
\[
  \theta,\phi \leftarrow \theta,\phi - \alpha\,\nabla_{\theta,\phi}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PPO}}.
\]

\subsection{Objective}

\subsubsection{Clipped policy objective}

The actor is updated with the clipped surrogate objective. Let $r_t(\theta)$ be the probability ratio between the new and the old policy on the sampled action:
\[
  r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}, \qquad r_t(\theta_{\mathrm{old}}) = 1.
\]
The clipped objective is then
\[
  L_t^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \min\!\left( r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}\bigl(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\bigr)\hat{A}_t \right).
\]
The ratio compares the new policy to the policy that collected the rollout, $\hat{A}_t$ signals whether the sampled action was better or worse than expected, and the clipping removes the incentive for overly large policy changes. ``Proximal'' means the update is restricted: PPO removes the incentive for probability ratios outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$.

\subsubsection{Value-function loss}

The critic is trained as a regression problem, predicting the return target for each sampled state:
\[
  L_t^{\mathrm{VF}}(\phi) = \bigl(V_\phi(s_t) - V_t^{\mathrm{target}}\bigr)^2.
\]
A better critic yields better advantage estimates and reduces update variance.

\subsubsection{Entropy bonus and combined objective}

PPO also rewards stochastic policies to avoid collapsing to one action too early. The entropy bonus is
\[
  S[\pi_\theta](s_t) = -\sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)\log \pi_\theta(a\mid s_t),
\]
and the combined objective minimised in our implementation is the negative of
\[
  L_t^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta, \phi) = L_t^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) - c_1 L_t^{\mathrm{VF}}(\phi) + c_2 S[\pi_\theta](s_t),
\]
with value coefficient $c_1$ and entropy coefficient $c_2$.

\subsection{Advantage estimation}

The policy loss needs a signal for whether the sampled action was good. Instead of using raw return, PPO uses an \textbf{advantage estimate}, which compares an estimate of the action's return with the critic's baseline:
\[
  \hat{A}_t \approx \underbrace{\widehat{Q}_t}_{\text{rollout estimate}} - \underbrace{V_\phi(s_t)}_{\text{critic baseline}}.
\]
If $\hat{A}_t > 0$ the action was better than expected and is made more likely; if $\hat{A}_t < 0$ it is made less likely.

\subsubsection{Rollout return}

A direct estimate compares a discounted rollout return with the critic's current value estimate. For a rollout segment of length $T$ starting at position $t$,
\[
  G_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1} \gamma^l r_{t+l} + \gamma^{T-t} V_\phi(s_T), \qquad \hat{A}_t = G_t - V_\phi(s_t).
\]
This uses the real rewards from the sampled trajectory segment and \emph{bootstraps} with $V_\phi(s_T)$ because PPO uses fixed-length rollouts that may stop before the episode ends. The unobserved future after $s_T$ is replaced by the critic's estimate; if $s_T$ is terminal, that estimate is $0$. This estimate has low bias but can have high variance.

\subsubsection{Generalized Advantage Estimation}

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) smooths many temporal-difference (TD) errors into one advantage estimate:
\[
  \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \qquad \hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}} = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}.
\]
Here $\delta_t$ measures the one-step surprise relative to the critic, and $\lambda$ controls how far future TD errors influence $\hat{A}_t$. The critic target used later is $V_t^{\mathrm{target}} = \hat{A}_t + V(s_t)$. Expanding the sum,
\[
  \hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}} = \delta_t + (\gamma\lambda)\delta_{t+1} + (\gamma\lambda)^2\delta_{t+2} + \cdots,
\]
so small $\lambda$ relies more on the critic (lower variance, more bias) while large $\lambda$ approaches the Monte-Carlo return (lower bias, more variance). $\lambda=0$ gives the one-step TD estimate and $\lambda=1$ the rollout-return-like estimate.

\subsection{Clipped vs.\ unclipped objective}

PPO reuses the same rollout for several gradient steps, so the ratio $r_t(\theta)$ measures how much the new policy changed on the sampled action. The unclipped surrogate $L_t^{\mathrm{unclip}}(\theta)=r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t$ would push the policy as far as the gradient allows: nothing stops very large policy changes. The clipped surrogate
\[
  L_t^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \min\!\left( r_t\hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t \right)
\]
allows updates near the old policy but removes the incentive outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$, with $\epsilon$ controlling how conservative the update is.

\subsection{Training loop}

\subsubsection{One PPO iteration}

PPO alternates between collecting fresh on-policy data and reusing that data for a limited number of optimization steps. The data is \emph{on-policy}: collected with the old policy before the update, and the stored old log-probabilities define the ratio $r_t(\theta)$ during optimization.

Instead of one environment, PPO commonly runs $N$ environments in parallel for $T$ steps, giving a batch of $N\cdot T$ transitions per iteration. For each transition PPO stores $s_t, a_t, r_t, \text{done}_t, \log\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$, and $V_\phi(s_t)$.

\subsubsection{From rollout to training batch}

After rollout collection, the data is converted into supervised-style targets for the actor and critic. From the rewards we bootstrap from $V_\phi(s_T)$ if needed, compute advantages $\hat{A}_t$ using the Monte-Carlo return or GAE, and form the critic targets $V_t^{\mathrm{target}} = \hat{A}_t + V_\phi(s_t)$. The rollout of shape $N\times T$ is then flattened into a training batch of size $N\cdot T$, shuffled, split into mini-batches, and the advantages are optionally normalized before updates.

\subsubsection{Mini-batch updates}

PPO does not update once on the whole rollout. It shuffles the rollout batch, splits it into mini-batches, and performs several passes over the same data. For each epoch and mini-batch, the networks recompute $\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ and $V_\phi(s_t)$ with the current parameters, form the ratio
\[
  r_t(\theta) = \exp\!\bigl(\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\bigr),
\]
and optimize the policy loss, value loss, and entropy bonus with Adam. Reusing data improves sample efficiency, and clipping makes this reuse safer by limiting policy drift from $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$. After all updates, $\theta_{\mathrm{old}} \leftarrow \theta$ and a new rollout is collected.

\section{Methodology}

For the purposes of this paper, the PPO algorithm was implemented using PyTorch\footnote{\url{https://pytorch.org/}} as a configurable training loop, allowing many variations of the algorithm to be trained via command-line parameters. Evaluation was implemented as a separate step, loading a trained checkpoint and rolling it out on a fixed, reproducible set of CartPole-v1 episodes. Both were combined in a sweep script, which trains and evaluates one agent for every combination of a chosen set of hyperparameters and random seeds.

\subsection{Network architecture}

A single network architecture was tested for both the actor and critic network. Both networks were constructed as MLPs with 2 hidden layers using \(\tanh\) activation functions.

\subsection{Hyperparameter sweep}

The project code allows the most important hyperparameters to be configured. A sweep is done over the product of all configured parameters, doing one training run for every combination of parameters.
During training, metrics of the model's performance were continuously collected. The actual evaluation, however, was done separately after training.

To collect evaluation metrics, a fixed amount of CartPole-v1 episodes was run. For the presented results, this was a total of \(60\) episodes per agent.
The seeds used for the evaluation run were consistent over all configurations to ensure reproducibility.

Furthermore, the number of total steps done in the environment during training was made configurable on a per-sweep basis. The results presented in this paper will use a training budget of 1 million steps.

The following parameters were varied across runs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Clip range} $\epsilon \in \{0.1,\,0.2,\,0.3\}$
  \item \textbf{Advantage estimator} --- Monte Carlo or GAE with $\lambda \in \{0.90,\,0.95,\,0.97\}$
  \item \textbf{Rollout/update ratio} --- (rollout length, epochs) pairs: $64{:}4,\;128{:}4,\;256{:}4,\;128{:}8$
\end{itemize}

All other parameters were kept fixed for all runs, using constants found in Table~\ref{tab:fixed-params}.

\subsection{Metrics}

\subsubsection{Training metrics}

During each training update, a broader set of metrics was logged to monitor optimization dynamics and policy behavior throughout training. Metrics were tracked using Aim\footnote{\url{https://github.com/aimhubio/aim}}, an open-source experiment tracker, which recorded each value alongside the corresponding update or mini-batch step for later inspection.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Episode statistics} --- mean return and length of completed episodes, together with the fraction of episodes solved (return $\geq 475$)
  \item \textbf{Rollout statistics} --- mean and total reward, mean return, mean advantage, and mean value estimate collected per rollout
  \item \textbf{Loss components} --- total loss, policy loss, and value loss, logged for every mini-batch update
  \item \textbf{Policy statistics} --- policy entropy and the mean and standard deviation of the probability ratio $r_t(\theta)$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation metrics}

During the evaluation step a smaller set of metrics was collected. These were used to judge the model's performance after a full training run.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean episode return} --- average reward collected per episode; maximum is $500$ per default
  \item \textbf{Standard deviation} --- standard deviation of the episode returns within the evaluation run, to judge stability across evaluation episodes
  \item \textbf{Success rate} --- episode is defined as ''solved'' if return $\geq 475$
\end{itemize}
